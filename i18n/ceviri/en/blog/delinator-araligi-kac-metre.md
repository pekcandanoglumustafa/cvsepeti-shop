The answer to "how many delineators do I need" depends on the spacing decision. The wrong spacing either inflates the budget unnecessarily or leaves drivers unable to perceive the boundary.

## How is delineator spacing determined?

Three factors determine it: **speed**, **purpose** and **sight line**.

As drivers speed up, their field of view narrows and they need to perceive objects from further away. At high speed, sparse spacing is sufficient because the driver is already looking far ahead. At low speed and in pedestrian areas, dense spacing is needed because the boundary must be physically perceived.

| Application | Speed | Spacing |
|---|---|---|
| Car park, pedestrian area boundary | 0–20 km/h | 1.5–2 m |
| Residential complex, service road | 20–30 km/h | 2–3 m |
| Urban lane separation | 30–50 km/h | 3–4 m |
| Main arterial | 50–70 km/h | 4–6 m |
| Divided road, ring road | 70–90 km/h | 6–10 m |
| Motorway | 90+ km/h | 8–12 m |

## Where is spacing reduced?

At certain points the posts are placed more densely than the standard spacing:

**Junctions and start of turns** — spacing is halved so the boundary is clearly perceived. The first 10-15 metres are densely spaced.

**Traffic island noses** — the first three delineators are placed 1-1.5 metres apart, then normal spacing resumes. This emphasises the start of the island.

**Near pedestrian crossings** — dense spacing is needed to stop pedestrians stepping into the roadway.

**Blind spots and bends** — spacing is reduced where visibility is limited.

**Lane narrowing transitions** — spacing is gradually tightened in the transition zone to signal the narrowing to drivers.

## How do you calculate the quantity?

The formula is simple:

**Quantity = (Length ÷ Spacing) + 1**

Examples:

- 100 m urban lane separation, 3 m spacing → (100 ÷ 3) + 1 = **35 units**
- 60 m car park boundary, 2 m spacing → (60 ÷ 2) + 1 = **31 units**
- 500 m ring road separation, 8 m spacing → (500 ÷ 8) + 1 = **64 units**

Add 10-15% to the calculation for denser spacing at junctions and turning points.

Also leave a margin for spares. Vehicle impacts and damage are inevitable; stocking spares equal to 5% of the total for the first year is sensible.

## What happens if the spacing is too wide?

Drivers perceive the delineators as individual objects rather than a continuous boundary. The tendency to pass between them increases — especially for motorcycles and narrow vehicles.

Night-time visibility also drops. If headlights cannot illuminate several delineators at once, the driver cannot follow the line.

## What happens if the spacing is too narrow?

Costs rise unnecessarily. On a 100-metre installation, using 4-metre spacing instead of 2 metres reduces the quantity from 51 to 26 — halving material cost.

Very dense spacing also looks visually heavy and increases maintenance labour.

## Is spacing different in a chain system?

Yes. If delineators are to be linked with chain, spacing is determined by the chain length, usually 1.5-2 metres.

Because the chain forms a continuous visual line, it may seem that the delineators can be spaced further apart, but to keep the chain from sagging, spacing should not exceed 2 metres.

Chain systems are generally used for pedestrian guidance and area delimitation, not for vehicle traffic.

## Does height affect spacing?

Yes. A taller delineator is visible from further away, so it can be spaced more widely.

With a 30 cm delineator at 6-metre spacing, the line is not perceived. The same spacing works fine with a 100 cm delineator.

Rule of thumb: **spacing should not exceed 4-8 times the delineator height.** For a 75 cm delineator, 3-6 metre spacing is appropriate.
