In most places, car park layouts are done piecemeal — first a few wheel stops are bought, then a speed bump is added, then protectors are considered once a column gets scraped. Planned as a whole, it costs less and the result is more orderly.

This guide gives the materials, quantity calculation and installation sequence needed to lay out a car park from scratch.

What materials are needed for a car park layout?

There are five main groups:

1. Wheel stops — one per parking bay. They stop vehicles from pulling too far forward and entering the wall or pedestrian area.

2. Delineators or lane separators — separate driving aisles from parking areas and mark turning points.

3. Speed bumps — reduce speed before entrances and ramps.

4. Column corner protectors — protect column and wall corners in indoor car parks. They also prevent damage to vehicles.

5. Safety mirrors — provide visibility at blind spots, ramp exits and sharp turns.

In addition, floor marking paint, direction signs and numbering may be needed.

Critical decisions in product selection

Wheel stop length. If the parking bay is 2.3-2.5 m wide, a 180 cm stop fits perfectly but is costly. A 110 cm model placed in the centre is sufficient in most cases. If narrow vehicles passing to the side is a problem, move up to 180 cm.

Speed bump height. In a residential car park, 3 cm is enough — the target speed is 20 km/h. 5 cm is unnecessarily harsh, causes contact on low vehicles and leads to complaints.

Column protector type. Rubber models absorb impact and do not damage vehicles. Yellow-black reflective ones stand out in indoor car parks. If the column corner is sharp, use a corner protector; if it is a flat wall, use a wall protection strip.

Mirror diameter. A 60 cm mirror is effective up to 10-15 metres. For long ramps and large areas, 80 cm should be chosen.

Why does the installation sequence matter?

Installing in the wrong order leads to rework. The correct sequence is:

  1. Floor marking. First, parking bays and driving aisles are painted. Everything else is positioned according to these lines.
  2. Wheel stops. Alignment is easy while the lines are clear.
  3. Speed bumps. At the entrance and ramp area, once the direction of vehicle traffic is clear.
  4. Delineators. Driving aisle boundaries are finalised after the wheel stops are in place.
  5. Column protectors and mirrors. Blind spots become clear once traffic flow has settled.

The reason for leaving the mirrors until last: before the layout is set up, blind spots are guessed; after it is set up, they are seen.

Common mistakes in residential and apartment car parks

Placing the wheel stop too close to the wall. The vehicle's front overhang touches the wall. Leave at least 80 cm.

Placing the speed bump on a ramp. A vehicle stopped on the ramp struggles to pull away, and low vehicles scrape. The speed bump should be placed before the ramp, on level ground.

Using non-reflective materials. If lighting in an indoor car park is inadequate, a black wheel stop is invisible. Choose yellow-black.

Placing delineators in the vehicle path. A delineator that is constantly hit will not last even a year. Keep the boundary 30 cm inside the driving line.

Not buying everything at once. Piecemeal purchasing triples shipping costs, misses bulk discounts and creates colour and model inconsistencies.

Is a management plan or permit needed?

In residential and apartment car parks, changes to common areas require a decision by the board of flat owners. Under the Condominium Law (Kat Mülkiyeti Kanunu), a decision must be taken for changes to common areas.

There is no such requirement for commercial and workplace car parks; the business owner decides.

If work is to be carried out on a municipal road or pavement, a municipal permit is required.
