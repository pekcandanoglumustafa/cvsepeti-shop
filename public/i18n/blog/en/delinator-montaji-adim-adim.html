Installing a delineator is a simple job, but mistakes are made at three points: the wrong drill bit, an uncleaned hole and an overtightened screw. Any of the three can stop the delineator from lasting even a year.

This guide sets out the sequence used on site and the critical point at each step.

What materials are needed to install a delineator?

Tools:
- Hammer drill (rotary hammer for concrete)
- Ø16 mm masonry drill bit
- Hammer
- Socket wrench or nut driver bit (usually 17 mm)
- Tape measure, chalk or marker pen

Materials (per delineator):
- 3 × Ø16×90 mm plastic wall plugs
- 3 × Ø10×110 mm lag screws

Most delineators come with these screws and plugs. If not, they must be sourced separately — do not use non-standard sizes.

Installation steps

1. Layout plan and spacing

Lay the delineators out in position before installation, check the spacing and adjust if necessary. Once the holes are drilled, no corrections can be made.

Even out the spacing with a tape measure. Spacing done by eye looks irregular from a distance.

2. Marking

The delineator's base flange usually has three screw holes. With the delineator in position, mark these three points on the ground with chalk, a paint marker or a fine drill bit.

Lift the delineator and set it aside.

3. Drilling

Fit a Ø16 mm bit to the drill. This size is critical:

  • If a smaller bit is used, the plug will not go into the hole, and forcing it will break it
  • If a larger bit is used, the plug sits loose and the screw will not hold

The hole should be 90-100 mm deep — the length of the plug. Hold the drill perpendicular to the ground; an angled hole seats the plug crookedly.

4. Cleaning the hole

This is the most frequently skipped step and the one that causes the most problems. Dust and debris left in the hole prevent the plug from seating fully.

Insert and withdraw the drill bit a few times without running it, then clear the dust by blowing or with a compressor.

5. Driving in the plug

Place the plastic plug in the hole and drive it in with a hammer. The plug must be flush with the ground surface — if it protrudes, the delineator base will not sit flat; if it is recessed, the screw will not hold properly.

6. Screwing

Put the delineator back in place and align the holes. Tighten the lag screw with a socket wrench.

Do not overtighten. Stop when the screw starts to resist. Overtightening cracks the plastic base and strains the plug.

Which surfaces can it be installed on?

Asphalt — the most common. A hammer drill is sufficient. Asphalt softens in summer heat, so avoid overtightening.

Concrete — the strongest hold. Requires a rotary hammer drill; drilling takes longer.

Paving stones — problematic. A delineator mounted on a single stone moves when the stone moves. If possible, make the base span more than one stone.

Interlocking pavers — the same problem as paving stones. In addition, the paver may crack.

Is installation without screws possible?

There are three alternatives:

Weighted-base model. The delineator sits on a rubber base and stays in place by its own weight. The right choice for temporary applications.

Chemical anchor. A hole is drilled, epoxy is injected and a rod is inserted. It is stronger than screw fixing but requires curing time.

Polyurethane adhesive. Used on surfaces that cannot be drilled. Its strength is lower than screw fixing and it is not recommended for heavy traffic.

How long does installing a delineator take?

For an experienced person, 8-10 minutes on average on asphalt. 12-15 minutes on concrete.

A 100-unit job can be completed by two people in one day. If one drills and cleans while the other inserts plugs and screws, the time is shorter.

Post-installation check

Push the delineator by hand in two directions. If the base moves on the ground, the screws are loose or the plug has play.

Check once more after the first month. In a new installation the screws may settle, and another round of tightening may be needed.
